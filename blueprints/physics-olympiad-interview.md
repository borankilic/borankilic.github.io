## C. The questions themselves — the contrast with school

**6.** What makes an olympiad problem different?

> It gives you a real life scenario — such as a rotating pendulum system or a complex circuit — as well
> as dimensions or parameters of a system. From there on it's all up to you: analyze the system, make
> an initial guess how the system will behave, decide on which equations or laws to use, write out the
> equations meticulously and solve them algebraically (often this is the most time consuming but the
> easiest part), and finally interpret the results. **You are free, you are not directed.** And it's
> not a simple analysis or a reasoning which fundamental high school physics assesses whether you have
> it or not. Often it fuses multiple topics. They also take much longer to solve since they include
> multiple steps and you struggle with it a lot more — but it's much more rewarding, since you need to
> be able to perform all those steps and know your physics very well, not just apply a simple formula.

**7.** One problem, described well enough to put in the post.

> The wobbling washing machine. A washing machine was modeled as a cube with side length L with a
> cylindrical hole inside of radius R. There is a rectangular rod fixed from the center, rotating with
> an angular frequency ω. The question asked for the maximum ω until it starts wobbling.
>
> I had to figure out what "wobbling" meant in this context — which was one side of the cube losing
> contact with the ground. After writing the equations of motion, they were not analytically solvable.
> Depending on which regime ω is in, you got different curves; some of which two curves intersect, and
> in some they don't. **The critical thing was that at the critical condition, these two curves would
> be tangent to each other.** And this condition was easier to solve, since their derivatives were
> algebraically much simpler.
>
> This was an inspiring and beautiful question where you learn this complex multi-step thinking and
> creativity to tackle the problem.

**8.** What does olympiad physics reward instead?

> Analyzing the system correctly. Writing everything down neatly and carefully, and being able to carry
> out calculations with precision until the last step. One major difference is there is almost no
> numerical calculation in olympiad physics — you always work with symbolic letters, which is very
> convenient. It helps you not get distracted with specific numbers, and helps you generalize the
> results to similar systems.

## D. The struggle

**9.** Longest with one problem; what those days looked like.

> Being stuck in a problem is very common and necessary. There were lots of problems I simply couldn't
> solve. But I would often leave a problem and come back to it days, even weeks after.

**10.** Moves when stuck.

> Study backward. Usually answers would be provided but not the solutions. So I would think: hmm, why
> would the expression in the solution take this form — and I'd guess the equations that would lead to
> that answer. Then the solution, which is the important part, would be revealed.

**11.** Where stubbornness stopped being useful.

> After some while your brain rejects to look at the problem from different angles and you start
> insisting on your method, which I realized was really harmful. It's always much better to come back
> to the problem next morning with a fresh mind and develop new points of view.

**12.** Days that ended unsolved.

> There were lots of days like this. Although it's frustrating, days like these contribute to you the
> most.

## E. The dream

**13.** The dream, properly.

> It was a magnetron problem — the part of the microwave that generates the microwaves. I was trying to
> calculate the final velocity of electrons using simple equations of motion: writing down the electric
> and magnetic field, calculating radial and tangential speeds with complicated integrals.
>
> But in my dream, it came to me that **I could just use basic energy and momentum conservation laws.**
> I wrote it down after I woke up, in the middle of the night, and actually found the solution
> correctly.

**14.** Once or more than once?

> Multiple times, but this was the most influential one.

## F. The payoff

**15.** The joy of understanding a system completely.

> It feels relieving. After you understand this system completely, it's as if you can break everything
> into parts in your head, recombine them again, and **you own it.** It feels both powerful and
> fulfilling, as it saves you from the state of cluelessness that you were in before.

**16.** The triumph.

> It's a feeling of **relief and peace.** Like getting rid of a burden. And also it's enlightening.

**17.** Was that why you kept going?

> It was mostly this feeling, but also some logistical perks — extra credit on the university entrance
> exam, and a scholarship.

## G. Now

**18.** Olympiad habits in research today.

> During research I still want to derive everything concretely. I find it much harder to move forward
> with assumptions or equations I didn't derive, compared to my colleagues. Once everything is written
> down mathematically from first principles, then there is no more question mark, and I feel much more
> confident with the experiment I'm doing. It also helps me manipulate the experiment and improve it
> much more easily, since I can confidently guess what's going to happen — which I can derive
> mathematically — rather than trusting my intuitions or related research.

**19.** Advice to a student deciding.

> I would recommend it to people who are willing to do what it takes. It's definitely not for anyone.
> It can be very difficult and isolating at times.
